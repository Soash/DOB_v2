DawnDock

by Dawn of Bioinformatics Ltd.

Molecular docking and virtual screening, without the command line.

Five docking engines behind one window. Load a protein, find the pocket, dock a ligand or a whole library, then read the poses, scores and contacts in a 3D view and export the lot. One of the engines scores poses with a neural network trained on solved crystal structures.

Overview

What DawnDock does

DawnDock takes you from a raw structure file to ranked, inspected docking poses on your own computer. Everything happens in one window, in the order the work actually happens: receptor, ligands, binding site, dock, results, analysis. There is no command line to learn and nothing to install beside it.

View full size View full size View full size View full size View full size View full size
Docked pose, in 3D

Receptor. Open a PDB or mmCIF file, or fetch one from the RCSB by its ID. Chains, amino acids, waters and bound molecules are counted and listed, and before anything is prepared DawnDock spells out what will be removed and what will stay.

Ligands. Add ligands from SDF, MOL, MOL2, PDB or SMILES files, a CSV table, or a SMILES string typed straight in. Each one is built in 3D, with its molecular weight, LogP, TPSA and Lipinski check worked out for you.

Binding site. Put the search box around a bound ligand, around named residues, on an atom you click, or let DawnDock find the pockets for you. The centre and size are shown as numbers you can edit.

Dock. Choose one of the five engines, set how hard it should search, from Quick look to Thorough, and dock. Progress is live, and any run can be stopped at once.

Results. Poses are listed best first. GNINA's CNN pose score sits beside the engine's affinity, and every hydrogen bond, pi-stacking and hydrophobic contact is listed by residue and distance.

Analysis. The figure step. Choose which contacts and labels to draw, switch to a white background for a journal, and save the picture at the size it is drawn.

Receptor preparation

Open PDB or mmCIF, or fetch a structure from the RCSB by its four-letter ID. Remove waters, choose which bound molecules to keep, add hydrogens at a pH you pick, and write the PDBQT. Nothing is removed without saying so first.

Ligand preparation

SDF, MOL, MOL2, PDB, SMILES files and CSV tables, or a SMILES string typed in. 3D coordinates from RDKit, a force-field clean-up, and molecular weight, LogP, TPSA and Lipinski violations shown for whichever ligand you pick.

Finding the binding site

Set the box around named residues, around a bound ligand, by clicking an atom, or let DawnDock find the pockets for you. On six test structures the pocket search put the box on the right site every time.

Batch screening

Load a library, press start, and come back to a ranked table. A score distribution above it tells you whether anything in the library is actually good, or whether everything scored the same and the run says nothing.

Interactions, measured

Hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, metal coordination, pi-stacking, pi-cation, halogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, each listed with its residue and distance and drawn into the 3D view.

Figures and exports

A dedicated step for the figure, with a white background for journals. One button writes a ZIP holding every pose, the scores as CSV, the receptor-plus-pose complexes ready for PyMOL, and the log and config of every run.

Docking engines

Five engines, one window

Pick an engine on the Dock step, or run the same target through several and compare. They are listed best first, by how often each one puts the ligand where the crystal structure says it goes. Speed runs almost exactly the other way, so the one at the top is also the slowest.

Two of the five are ready the moment you install

AutoDock Vina and AutoDock 4 are included and work immediately — open a structure, dock, make a figure. Smina, GNINA and LeDock are Linux-only programs: they run through WSL, and DawnDock fetches and sets them up for you from inside the program when you ask for them. If WSL is missing it offers to install it, and says plainly that the two bundled engines work without it.

AI docking engine

Learns what a correct pose looks like from thousands of solved structures, and scores poses with that network.

GNINA Neural network Needs WSL

Smina plus a convolutional neural network that rescores every pose it generates, reported as a CNN pose score and a CNN affinity. It is the slowest engine here, and the one to reach for when getting the pose right matters more than finishing quickly. It always runs on the processor, so no graphics card is needed.

Standard docking engines

Add up physical terms — sterics, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contact, torsional strain — with weights fitted to measured data. Far faster, and directly comparable with the published literature.

2 Smina Needs WSL

A fork of AutoDock Vina with extra scoring functions and far finer control over minimisation. Its Vinardo function poses better than stock Vina.

3 AutoDock Vina v1.2.7 Included

The standard engine, and the baseline the others are measured against. Fast, well validated, the one most papers cite, and the quickest of the five.

4 AutoDock 4 v4.2.6 Included

The classic. Precomputed energy grids searched by a genetic algorithm, so it is much slower, but it reports a clustering of its runs that tells you how reproducible a pose was — something none of the others can.

5 LeDock v1.0 Needs WSL

A different lineage entirely: simulated annealing with an evolutionary search, and a scoring function fitted to crystal poses rather than affinities. When it agrees with Vina you have two independent opinions. Academic use only.

Not sure which to pick?

Dock with AutoDock Vina for a fast answer, then press Rescore with GNINA on the poses worth a closer look. Rescoring takes the poses already found and scores them again with the network, without searching the box a second time — about nine seconds for five poses, against seventy for a full GNINA run. You get the speed of one engine and the judgement of the other, and both sets of numbers appear side by side rather than one quietly replacing the other.

Validation

How accurate is it

Every engine was run over the whole Astex Diverse Set — 85 crystal structures, each ligand rebuilt from its published SMILES, one box rule for all of them, and DawnDock's own default settings. A redocking error under 2 Å is the usual criterion for a correct result.

Astex Diverse Set, run in this project. "Right pocket" means the pose landed on the correct site even when the orientation was off.
Engine Top pose under 2 Å Best of 3 Right pocket Median error
GNINA (neural network) 80.5% 90.2% 98.8% 0.94 Å
Smina 56.1% 72.0% 87.8% 1.71 Å
AutoDock Vina 52.4% 72.6% 88.1% 1.75 Å
AutoDock 4 52.4% 62.2% 84.1% 1.83 Å
LeDock 50.0% 63.1% 79.8% 1.99 Å

Neural-network scoring placed 80.5% of ligands within 2 Å of the crystal pose, against 52.4% for a classical scoring function on the same structures, with the same boxes and the same ligand preparation. More than half of GNINA's answers land under 1 Å.

Read it as a starting point, not a verdict

This compares the engines DawnDock ships against each other. It is not a comparison with any other docking program. On your own target the order can differ, and running two engines and comparing them is worth more than trusting any one of them.

An engine's affinity is a scoring-function estimate, not a measured binding free energy. These functions rank and pose reasonably well; they do not predict potency reliably. Virtual screening has not been benchmarked here — the figures above are redocking accuracy, which is a different question from ranking actives against decoys.

Every pose, score and RMSD behind this table is published, one file per engine, so the numbers can be recomputed rather than taken on trust: the per-complex data on GitHub, and the full write-up in the archived record on Zenodo.

How much space DawnDock itself takes

About 1 GB: the installer is 298 MB and unpacks to roughly 740 MB. Adding the three optional engines through WSL needs several GB more, most of it GNINA, so the drive sizes above leave room for that and for your own structures and results.

No administrator rights

DawnDock installs for you alone, into your own user folder, so Windows never asks for a password.

Nothing else to install

No Python, no compiler, no separate toolkit. Everything the program needs is inside the installer.

No internet needed to dock

Only to download the installer, to fetch a structure from the RCSB by ID, to check for updates, or to add the optional engines.

Installing

Four steps, and an optional fifth

  1. 1

    Download the installer

    Use the Download for Windows button. You will get DawnDock-Setup-1.0.1.exe, about 298 MB.

  2. 2

    Run it

    Double-click the file. Windows will not ask for an administrator password, because DawnDock installs into your own user folder.

  3. 3

    If Windows warns you about the publisher

    You will probably see “Windows protected your PC”. Choose More info, then Run anyway. This appears because DawnDock is not yet signed with a paid code-signing certificate, not because anything is wrong with the file. If you would rather check the download first, the next section shows you how.

  4. 4

    Open DawnDock

    A Start-menu entry and an optional desktop shortcut are created, and an uninstaller is registered, so removing it later works from Settings → Apps like any other program. The first launch shows a splash screen while the 3D view and the chemistry toolkits load.

    At this point AutoDock Vina and AutoDock 4 are ready. You can open a structure, prepare it, dock and make your figure without doing anything else.

  5. 5

    Optional: add GNINA, Smina and LeDock

    These three are published only as Linux programs, so they run through WSL (Windows Subsystem for Linux). You need this step only if you want them — and GNINA, the neural-network engine, is one of them.

    Open Run → Docking engines (Ctrl+E). The dialog says what WSL is, shows what your machine already has, and fetches and sets up each engine for you, then runs it once to prove it works. Nothing needs pip, curl or a password.

If WSL is not installed yet

The same dialog offers to run it for you. Doing it by hand instead is one command in an administrator PowerShell, followed by a restart:

wsl --install

It needs administrator rights, a restart, and virtualisation switched on in your machine's BIOS or UEFI. If that setting is off, no program can turn it on, and the dialog tells you so rather than failing quietly.

GNINA is a CUDA build, so its download is large — the binary and the libraries it names come to several GB, fetched in one step. It still runs on the processor afterwards, with no graphics card needed.

Trust

Checking your download

Every release is published with two small files beside the installer: a SHA-256 checksum, and an Ed25519 signature over that checksum made with the author's private key. A matching checksum tells you the download is intact. A valid signature tells you who made it. Both are independent of Windows' own publisher check.

The checksum

In PowerShell, in your Downloads folder:

Get-FileHash .\DawnDock-Setup-1.0.1.exe -Algorithm SHA256

It should read:

6ac85686171fa845533adbd487482977bcc01a7821cb6fce1707fcabed27dd8f

The signature

Download verify_authorship.py and the installer's .sig from the same release, then:

pip install cryptography python verify_authorship.py --installer DawnDock-Setup-1.0.1.exe

The script carries the public key, so it needs nothing else. It prints the key's fingerprint on every run so you can compare it against the copy published in the repository:

SHA256:g7QtjQ1qYTFPg4735rpfVcUnLau1sb+nRvl58Fvh76g

Download them from this site: the script, the signature, the checksum file and the public key. The same set is published in the GitHub repository and the Zenodo record, so you can compare the key against a copy that did not come from this site.

Questions

Questions people ask

Is DawnDock free?

Yes, for academic, nonprofit, educational and personal research. There is nothing to pay and no account to make.

Commercial use — by companies, or for paid services, consulting or commercial R&D — needs written permission first. Write to dawn.of.bioinformatics@gmail.com. DawnDock is licensed rather than sold, and it is not open source. You may publish what you find; please cite it.

Windows blocked the installer. What do I do?

Choose More info, then Run anyway. Windows shows this for any installer that is not signed with a paid code-signing certificate, which DawnDock is not yet.

If you would rather not take that on trust, check the checksum and the signature first. That is exactly what they are published for.

Do I need WSL?

Not to use DawnDock. AutoDock Vina and AutoDock 4 are included as Windows programs, and everything around them — preparation, the 3D view, interaction analysis, batch docking, the figures and every export — is part of DawnDock itself. On a machine that has never heard of WSL you can open a structure, prepare it, dock and make your figure.

WSL is only needed for the three Linux-only engines: Smina, GNINA and LeDock. The engine dialog explains what WSL is, offers to install it for you, and says plainly that the bundled engines work right now without it.

Do I need an NVIDIA graphics card for the AI engine?

No. GNINA always runs on the processor in DawnDock, deliberately, so there is no CUDA driver to get wrong and no class of failure that only appears on some machines. It costs speed: measured on a laptop with no CUDA at all, GNINA docked imatinib into Abl kinase in about 70 seconds, and rescoring five existing poses took 9 seconds.

Which file formats does it read?

Receptors: PDB and mmCIF, gzipped or not, or fetched from the RCSB by its four-character ID.

Ligands: SDF, MOL, MOL2, PDB, SMILES files and CSV tables with a SMILES column, or a SMILES string typed straight in.

What comes out: poses as SDF and in the engine's own format, receptor-plus-pose complexes as PDB ready for PyMOL, every score as CSV, PNG figures, and one ZIP holding all of it with the logs and configs.

Does my structure get uploaded anywhere?

No. Docking runs on your own computer, and your structures and ligands are never sent anywhere. DawnDock uses the network for three things only, all of them at your request: downloading a structure from the RCSB when you type a PDB ID, downloading the optional engines, and checking whether a newer version has been published.

Does it run on macOS or Linux?

Not yet. Version 1.0.1 is a Windows application, built and tested on Windows 10 and 11, 64-bit.

Why does the same ligand sometimes score differently?

Docking engines search at random, so in general they can. DawnDock pins the random seed, so docking the same ligand twice with the same settings gives exactly the same answer. The one exception is LeDock, which exposes no seed at all; across five repeats of the same run its scores spanned about 0.25 kcal/mol, and the Dock step says so on its card rather than leaving you to discover it.

Something went wrong. What should I send?

Open Help → Copy diagnostics. It puts the version, your results folder, every engine it found with its version, and anything that would not start onto the clipboard. Paste that into your message.

If the program closed unexpectedly there is also a log at %APPDATA%\DawnDock\crash.log. Attach it, say what you were doing, and share the structure and ligand if you are able to — that helps most of all.

Citing

How to cite DawnDock

If DawnDock helped with work you publish, please cite it:

Ahmed, S. F. (2026). DawnDock: a multi-engine GUI application for molecular docking with deep learning scoring (Version 1.0.1) [Computer software]. Zenodo. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24198678

10.5281/zenodo.24198678

That DOI belongs to this exact version and never moves, so a paper points at the code that produced its results. Cite the version you actually ran. This is the wording Zenodo gives for the record itself. The program's own About window, where the citation can be copied straight out, and CITATION.cff name Dawn of Bioinformatics Ltd. as the publisher instead of Zenodo. Both forms are accepted; use whichever your journal prefers.

A link that stays current

Zenodo also gives DawnDock one DOI that covers every version and always resolves to the newest: 10.5281/zenodo.24185576. Use it where you mean the software in general — a bookmark, a link from a website, a software list — because it will not go stale when the next version comes out. In a paper, use the version DOI above instead: a reader needs to know which version produced the results, and this one will point at a later release than the one you ran.

Please also cite the engine you used

DawnDock runs these programs; it does not replace them, and their authors deserve the credit. The manual lists the full references, and so does the program's About window.
